# FAO Wood Gas as Engine Fuel – Key Design Guidelines (English Units)

Source: FAO Forestry Department, 1986 – "Wood Gas as Engine Fuel"  
Original document: https://www.fao.org/4/T0512E/T0512e00.htm  
Chapter 2.5 Design of downdraught gasifiers

These are the classic empirical rules the skill is built on. All values have been converted to English (US customary) units.

## Hearth Load Concept

Hearth load (B_g) is defined as the volume of producer gas (standard conditions) divided by the cross-sectional area of the throat (narrowest constriction).

- Maximum continuous hearth load: approximately 2.95 scf/h per square inch of throat area  
  (original: 0.9 m³/h·cm²)
- Minimum for acceptable tar levels in normal Imbert designs: about 1.0–1.15 scf/h per in²  
  (original: 0.30–0.35 m³/h·cm²)
- Better-insulated modern designs can go lower (down to ~0.5–0.6 scf/h per in²)
- Typical turndown ratio: 2.5–3

Gas yield approximation used in the original: 1 kg dry fuel ≈ 2.5 m³ gas  
→ approximately 40 scf per pound of dry wood (we use a slightly more conservative 35–40 scf/lb in the skill).

## Design Procedure Summary (from FAO)

1. Estimate the maximum gas flow required by the engine (or heating load).
2. Choose a design hearth load (usually near the upper end for maximum size, e.g. 2.3–2.6 scf/h·in²).
3. Calculate required throat area and diameter.
4. Size the remaining geometry from the empirical rules below.

## Geometry Rules (converted)

- Nozzle air inlet velocity: 100–115 ft/s (original 30–35 m/s)
- Throat inclination (cone angle): 45–60°
- Hearth diameter at air-inlet height:
  - Single-throat design: throat diameter + ~4 inches
  - Double-throat / Imbert design: throat diameter + ~8 inches
- Height of reduction zone: minimum 8 inches (original >20 cm). Historical average was about 12.6 inches (32 cm).
- Height of nozzle plane above the narrowest constriction: about 4 inches (original 10 cm) as a starting point; actual values vary with size (see original figures).

## Important Notes from the Original Text

- The rules are based on experience with relatively uniform wood blocks.
- Good insulation of the hot zone improves the turndown ratio (allows lower minimum hearth load without tar).
- The oxidation zone must be hot and uniform to crack tars. Cold spots cause tar problems.
- A movable grate is recommended so the charcoal bed in the reduction zone can be stirred to prevent blockages.
